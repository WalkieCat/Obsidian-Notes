# Enterprise System architecture
1. Typical modules available in an ERP and their potential benefits are:
	1. **Production**: Helps in planning and optimizing manufacturing process to
		1. Benefit: Ensure that all products are used to to meet production goals
	2. **Purchasing**: Streamlines the acquiring process of raw materials and supplies. 
		1. Benefit: Cost saving by automatically converting orders and allows the use of electric catalog for products
	3. **Assets management**: Maintaining appropriate stock level in warehouses. 
		1. Benefit: Enhance productivity of incoming and outgoing goods and reduce overstocking
	4. **Sales and Marketing**: Managing order placement, scheduling, shipping and handling invoices.
		1. Benefits: Help the business stays informed about customer relationships, track customer history and understand customer relations
	5. **HR**: Management of human resources and capitals
		1. Benefits: Developing internal talents to align with corporate objectives, increase in efficiency using standardized process and simplify other processes

2. The process of choosing which architecture to use when implementing and ERP is difficult because the organization needs to balance the investment with long term consequences. 
   
   The organization needs to identify their ability to adapt to different needs or changes of the organization itself. These overtime can change drastically, and sometime warrant the restructure of the entire ERP system. Additionally, the organization needs to consider what type of hardware do they need, since all architecture have vastly different requirements that might not fit the purpose of the organization. Furthermore, stakeholders might disagree over the chosen ERP and its different aspect. This can be due to a lack of technical understanding of the ERP itself, and thus leading to delays in integration and thus reducing the efficiency of the company.

## TechnologyOne case study

### Q1:
In the Research and Approach Plan, the main lesson was to identify a clear mission and you have to answer some questions: what, why, how, who are you doing this for, who are you asking and how are you asking. The team wanted to replace the Minute Manger because it is not suitable for cloud technology. They identified industries that needed a formal meeting process and find an early adopter to test out the new software. They gathered data by watching the live broadcasted council meeting.

From the High Level Business Process, the main reason was to understand the business process at the high level and create a navigation point for the project path. The key detail was to see in an ideal world how the project would succeed first and then see the negative path to see where the project fails. This detailed map reveal that the software could not replace all human steps, and that someone need to contribute their effort into the program. 

### Q2:
A persona is a contributor to the business process, and it is recommended to include personality traits to these personas to see how we can lower their resistance to change. 

The personas presented are:
- Meeting organizers: Create and schedule meetings, agendas and minutes, view and approve submissions. Personality traits are methodical and organized
- Meeting owner: View and approve draft agendas, submissions and minutes
- Submitters: Able to create submissions for the scheduled meeting
- Members: Able to view all scheduled meeting but only able to view the approved agendas and submission
- Attendee: A special guest who might be invited to the meeting and can only view the approved agenda, submissions and minutes of said meeting
- Action assignee: Person who received an action to complete

### Q3:
The UX testing phase shows the importance of getting a mock live in front of your early adopters. In the case study, early users could not find the option to create a new meeting. This process shows that the software that you envisioned might be intuitive to you, but not to the user themselves. So for the DXP to be easy to use, you have to ask your customers what they want to do, and then iterate on your current software until everything make sense to anyone that you put the product in front of them. The most important lesson is to listen to your customer feedback, and make all information clear to the user to prevent the user from confusing themselves.

### Q4:
To actually prove the product that you are designing is achieving what you want to do, there are some steps that you can take:
- **Deploy early**: Deploy the software as soon as it is functional so the customers can get used to the systems and provide you with feedback
- **Include developers into regular meeting**: Include all people who worked on the software to hear the users' frustration first hand, so that they can have an idea on how to tackle these problems
- **Maintain live feedback**: Maintain a live feedback platform where user can voice their opinions and rank these opinions on a one to three scale - with one being go live requirement, two being what they want but they have a workaround and three being future enhancements. And the developers can discuss these opinions on check-ins opportunity
- **Run the DXP in parallel with current system**: Doing this allows the provider to avoid "surprises" when the product goes live
- **Conduct retrospective**: Sit with the early adopters to discuss what went right at the start, what they value and ask what can be improved next time.
